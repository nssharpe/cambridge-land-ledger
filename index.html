Cambridge, Massachusetts · FY2026 assessments

Who holds Cambridge's land value?

The City assesses every parcel's land and its buildings separately. Cambridge's land is worth about $31.6 billion, and about $805 million of it sits under surface parking on taxable parcels. This site maps that land, asks whether it is valued accurately, and tests what a land-weighted property tax would do to tax bills here.

02 · How it works

Two lots, two tax rules

Today one rate applies to land and buildings alike. A building exemption lowers the tax on buildings and raises the rate on everything that remains taxable, which is mostly land. The class still raises the same total.

Two lots on the same street

Picture two 10,000 sq ft commercial lots, each with $1.2 million of land. One holds a surface parking lot. The other holds a $6.8 million building. Today the building lot pays about 6.5 times as much tax. Exempt half of every building's value and the commercial rate rises to keep the class total unchanged: the parking lot's bill goes up by more than half, and the building lot's bill goes down.

The same arithmetic runs through homes. A parcel pays more when its land share of value is above its class average. In Cambridge, single-family houses sit on a lot of land per dollar of building, and condo buildings on little, so houses pay more and condos pay less.

03 · Calculator

What would change?

Pick how much of every building's value to exempt. Each tax class still raises exactly its FY2026 total, so the rate rises as the building exemption grows. Owner-occupants keep the residential exemption, recalculated on the new values; that keeps each owner-occupant's saving near today's $3,400. Every one of the 25,400 taxable properties is recomputed in your browser.

At a 50% building exemption, the median owner-occupied single-family home pays 9.5% more (about $741 a year), and the median condo pays 9.3% less. A land value tax in Cambridge would shift tax from houses to condos. It would not cut the typical homeowner's bill.

Which classes
Residential exemption

About the 35% option. A larger residential exemption gives every owner-occupant a bigger deduction. Rental and investor property pays for it. Any homeowner gain in this setting comes from the bigger exemption. The land/building change on its own still moves tax from owner-occupied houses toward condos.

Commercial property included. Labs, with costly buildings on their land, pay less. Small retail stores, restaurants and parking lots pay more. That is why the plan recommends residential-only coverage if Cambridge ever opts in.

04 · Elsewhere in Massachusetts

Other Massachusetts cities

We ran the same 50% building exemption on Worcester, Springfield, Fitchburg and Leominster using state parcel data. Vacant and parking land pays 57% to 72% more in every city. Two- and three-family homes, condos and apartments pay less. The median single-family home pays 0% to 3.3% more, about $0 to $170 a year. No Massachusetts city we tested shows the median single-family homeowner paying less.

The reason is structural. In Massachusetts cities, single-family homes carry the highest land share of value in the residential class, and vacant and parking land is too small a share of the tax base to fund a homeowner cut.

05 · Track A

Questions for the Assessing Department

Accurate land values need no new law, and they matter more under any land-weighted tax. These are questions. Each points to a pattern in public data that Assessing may well be able to explain. Dollar effects should wait for the Department's answers and confirmation from the state Division of Local Services.

  1. Commercial and residential land schedules. Comparing parcels of the same size in the same tax district and zoning, commercial and industrial land is assessed about 37% lower per square foot than residential land (95% interval: 29% to 43% lower). What explains the difference, and which land schedules apply to each class? Consistent across tests
  2. Parking and vacant land. Land classed as parking lots is assessed 27% to 50% below other commercial land of the same size nearby, depending on how the comparison is set up; the intervals run from 10% to 70% below. Would Assessing review parking and vacant parcels against comparable land? Fragile result
  3. Surface parking on other parcels. OpenStreetMap shows 122 acres of surface parking on taxable parcels, and only 13 of those acres are on parcels classed as parking lots. Shopping centers, offices and labs hold most of it. How does the land schedule treat large surface lots attached to other uses? Map data, volunteer-drawn
  4. Condo land. Condo units show $0 land value in the open data. Would Assessing publish each unit's land value, allocated by percentage interest? In small condo buildings our estimate of land share is about a quarter below comparable two- and three-family houses, so it is probably low. Data gap

Why the answers matter to residents

Under FY2026 rules, correcting commercial land values would move tax within the commercial class only. The City projects that from FY2028 commercial property will pay the legal maximum: 175% of its share of total value. If so, a real gap in commercial land values would matter to residents: correcting the full gap could lower the residential levy by up to about $38 million a year. Fixing parking lots alone would move less than $1 million. Upper bound, pending state confirmation

06 · Track B

What a state law could do

The legal limits

Cambridge cannot adopt a land value tax by itself. The state constitution lets the Legislature tax property by use class, and requires equal treatment inside each class:

“…the general court may classify real property according to its use in no more than four classes and to assess, rate and tax such property differently in the classes so established, but proportionately in the same class, and except that reasonable exemptions may be granted.”Mass. Const. Amend. Art. 112

The Home Rule Amendment keeps the taxing power with the state:

“Nothing in this article shall be deemed to grant to any city or town the power to … levy, assess and collect taxes”Mass. Const. Amend. Art. 89, §7

All four use classes are already in use, so land and buildings cannot become separate classes. The only opening is the “reasonable exemptions” clause: a law that exempts part of every building's value. No court has tested that. Our informed guess is a 25% to 40% chance it would survive review by the Supreme Judicial Court. That estimate is a judgment call. It is not legal advice.

Two asks for the Legislature

1. Expand the Urban Center Housing TIF (c.40 §60). Cities can already exempt the added value of new housing for up to 20 years, inside approved commercial-center zones, one project at a time, if at least 15% of the units are affordable at 80% of area median income (or 25% at 110%). A bill could let cities offer standard, by-right exemptions for new housing across more of the city. It rests on an existing statute, so it has the firmest legal footing. Exemptions tied to construction dates carry their own fairness risk, so the bill should stay close to the TIF structure.

2. A local-option building exemption. Any city could choose to exempt a share of building value, with these safeguards:

  • the city chooses which classes it covers;
  • class shares of the levy are set on values before the exemption, so commercial owners cannot shift tax onto residents;
  • the residential exemption is recalculated in proportion;
  • single-rate towns may pool all classes;
  • a phase-in and a hardship deferral.

Its case is density and fairness: idle land pays more, while triple-deckers, condos and apartments pay less. In Cambridge it would raise the typical single-family bill, so we do not propose that Cambridge adopt it now.
