Cambridge, Massachusetts · FY2026 assessments

Who holds Cambridge's land value?

The City assesses every parcel's land and buildings separately, and all together Cambridge's land is worth about $30.7 billion. About $805 million of that is land under surface parking on taxable parcels. I built this site to map that land and see what a land-weighted property tax would actually do to tax bills here (and along the way, to ask whether the land is being valued accurately).

Full disclosure, I'm Nate Sharpe, I built this site, and I live in a condo here in Cambridge. Condos would pay less under the scenarios shown.

02 · How it works

Two lots, two tax rules

Right now one rate applies to land and buildings alike. A building exemption lowers the tax on buildings, so the rate has to go up on everything that's still taxable (which is mostly land), and each class still raises the same total.

Two lots on the same street

Picture two 10,000 sq ft commercial lots on the same street, each with $1.2 million of land. One is a surface parking lot and the other has a $6.8 million building on it. Today the building lot pays about 6.5 times as much tax. If you exempt half of every building's value, the commercial rate goes up to keep the class total the same, so the parking lot's bill goes up by more than half and the building lot's bill goes down.

The same math applies to homes. A parcel pays more when land is a bigger share of its value than the average for its class. In Cambridge, single-family houses have a lot of land per dollar of building and condo buildings have very little, so houses pay more and condos pay less.

03 · Calculator

What would change?

Pick how much of every building's value to exempt. Each tax class still raises exactly what it raised in FY2026, so the rate goes up as the building exemption grows. Owner-occupants keep the residential exemption, recalculated on the new values, which keeps each owner-occupant's savings close to today's $3,400. All 25,400 taxable properties get recomputed right in your browser.

At a 50% building exemption, the median owner-occupied single-family home pays 12% more (about $920 a year), and the median condo pays 13% less. So in Cambridge, a land value tax would mostly shift tax from houses to condos, and it wouldn't cut the typical homeowner's bill.

Which classes
Residential exemption

About the 35% option. A bigger residential exemption gives every owner-occupant a bigger deduction, and rental and investor property pays for it. So any homeowner gain in this setting comes from the bigger exemption. The land/building change by itself still moves tax from owner-occupied houses toward condos.

Commercial property included. Labs, which have expensive buildings on their land, pay less. Small shops, restaurants and parking lots pay more. That's why I'd recommend residential-only coverage if Cambridge ever opts in.

04 · Elsewhere in Massachusetts

Other Massachusetts cities

I ran the same 50% building exemption on Worcester, Springfield, Fitchburg and Leominster using state parcel data. In every city, vacant and parking land pays 57% to 72% more, and two- and three-family homes, condos and apartments pay less. The median single-family home pays 0% to 3.3% more (about $0 to $170 a year). I didn't find a Massachusetts city where the median single-family homeowner pays less.

That's because in Massachusetts cities, single-family homes have the highest land share of value in the residential class, and vacant and parking land is too small a share of the tax base to pay for a cut for homeowners.

05 · Track A

Questions for the Assessing Department

Getting land values right doesn't take a new law, and it matters even more under any land-weighted tax. I'm asking these as questions, since each one points to a pattern in public data that Assessing may well be able to explain. I'd hold off on dollar effects until the Department answers and the state Division of Local Services confirms the math.

  1. Commercial and residential land schedules. When I compare parcels of the same size, tax district and zoning, commercial and industrial land is assessed about 37% lower per square foot than residential land (95% interval 29% to 43% lower). What explains the difference, and which land schedules apply to each class? Consistent across tests
  2. Parking and vacant land. Land classed as parking lots is assessed 27% to 50% below other commercial land of the same size nearby, depending on how I set up the comparison (the intervals run from 10% to 70% below). Would Assessing review parking and vacant parcels against comparable land? Fragile result
  3. Surface parking on other parcels. OpenStreetMap shows 122 acres of surface parking on taxable parcels, and only 13 of those acres are on parcels classed as parking lots. Most of it belongs to shopping centers, offices and labs. How does the land schedule treat big surface lots attached to other uses? Map data, volunteer-drawn
  4. Condo land. Condo units show $0 land value in the open data. Could Assessing publish each condo building's land value, and each unit's share of it by percentage interest? My estimate checks out well against the land the Assessor carried on lots before they were converted to condos (typically within 4%), but for large buildings it's an extrapolation (typically within about 20%). Data gap

Why the answers matter to residents

Under FY2026 rules, correcting commercial land values would only move tax around within the commercial class. But the City projects that starting in FY2028, commercial property will pay the legal maximum (175% of its share of total value). If that happens, a real gap in commercial land values would matter to residents, since correcting the full gap could lower the residential levy by up to about $38 million a year. Fixing parking lots alone would move less than $1 million. Upper bound, pending state confirmation

06 · Track B

What a state law could do

The legal limits

Cambridge can't adopt a land value tax on its own. The state constitution lets the Legislature tax property by use class, but it requires equal treatment inside each class:

“…the general court may classify real property according to its use in no more than four classes and to assess, rate and tax such property differently in the classes so established, but proportionately in the same class, and except that reasonable exemptions may be granted.”Mass. Const. Amend. Art. 112

The Home Rule Amendment keeps the taxing power with the state:

“Nothing in this article shall be deemed to grant to any city or town the power to … levy, assess and collect taxes”Mass. Const. Amend. Art. 89, §7

All four use classes are already taken, so land and buildings can't become separate classes. That leaves the “reasonable exemptions” clause, meaning a law that exempts part of every building's value. No court has tested that, and my best guess is a 25% to 40% chance it would survive review by the Supreme Judicial Court, but I'm not a lawyer.

Two asks for the Legislature

1. Expand the Urban Center Housing TIF (c.40 §60). Cities can already exempt the added value of new housing for up to 20 years, but only inside approved commercial-center zones, one project at a time, and only if at least 15% of the units are affordable at 80% of area median income (or 25% at 110%). A bill could let cities offer standard, by-right exemptions for new housing across more of the city. Since it builds on an existing statute, it's on the firmest legal footing. Exemptions tied to construction dates have their own fairness problems, though, so I'd keep the bill close to the TIF structure.

2. A local-option building exemption. Any city could choose to exempt a share of building value, with these safeguards:

  • the city chooses which classes it covers;
  • class shares of the levy are set on values before the exemption, so commercial owners cannot shift tax onto residents;
  • the residential exemption is recalculated in proportion;
  • single-rate towns may pool all classes;
  • a phase-in and a hardship deferral.

The case for it is density and fairness, since idle land pays more and triple-deckers, condos and apartments pay less. In Cambridge it would raise the typical single-family bill, though, so I'm not proposing that Cambridge adopt it now.
